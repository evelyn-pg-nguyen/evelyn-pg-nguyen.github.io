Automating Bank Reconciliation with AI

Overview

A Python tool I built with Claude Code to automate bank reconciliation, match transactions and highlight the exceptions that actually need review.

Business Problem

Bank reconciliation was repetitive and time-consuming. The process required manually comparing bank transactions with accounting records, checking timing differences and investigating anything that did not match.

The real question was: how can I reduce the manual work without losing the controls that make a reconciliation reliable?

What I Built

I defined the accounting logic and used Claude Code to help build a Python workflow that:

  • standardises the input files
  • matches transactions based on defined rules
  • considers timing differences
  • separates matched transactions from exceptions
  • produces a review-ready reconciliation file

How It Works

The tool takes the bank statement and accounting ledger as inputs, standardises both datasets and applies the matching logic. Transactions that meet the rules are identified automatically, while anything unusual is left for review.

Instead of manually checking every transaction, I can focus on the exceptions.

Controls & Validation

Automation does not mean removing the review.

The output keeps the original transaction information and shows what was matched, what was not matched and the result of the reconciliation. This makes it possible to trace the output back to the source data and manually spot-check the result.

Impact

The original reconciliation could take hours of repetitive checking. The automated process runs in seconds, so most of the time can instead be spent investigating the transactions that actually need judgement.

Automating Cohort Analysis with AI

Overview

A Python-based cohort analysis I built with AI assistance to turn three separate data exports into one repeatable retention model, covering customer retention, revenue retention, churn and product performance.

Business Problem

For a subscription business, looking only at monthly revenue does not tell you whether customers are actually staying.

The data was also spread across different sources. Payments showed the money, subscriptions showed the product, and accounting data helped fill some of the gaps. Rebuilding the analysis manually every month would take too much time and make the logic difficult to keep consistent.

The question was: how can I turn several messy data sources into a cohort model that is repeatable, traceable and easy to understand?

What I Built

I defined the cohort, retention and churn logic, then used AI to help me build a Python workflow that:

  • keeps only actual paid invoices
  • combines Stripe payment and subscription data
  • uses accounting data as a fallback for missing product information
  • groups customers by their first paid month
  • identifies active and churned customers
  • calculates customer and revenue retention by relative month
  • compares retention by product
  • produces a formatted Excel workbook automatically

The script uses paid Stripe invoices as the main revenue source and includes both Stripe-processed and out-of-band payments.

How It Works

The workflow first cleans and joins the three datasets.

Each customer is assigned to a cohort based on the month of their first paid invoice. From there, the model stops looking at customers by calendar month and instead compares them by relative month, such as M0, M1, M2 and M3.

This allows customers who started at different times to be compared on the same timeline.

The model then creates both customer-count and revenue-retention matrices. Product-level cohort views are also created so retention can be compared across different services.

Churn Logic

One of the most important parts was defining churn clearly instead of leaving it as an assumption.

The model treats a customer as churned when either:

  • their recorded service period has ended and there is no later paid invoice, or
  • no service-period information exists and there has been no paid invoice for more than 31 days.

Otherwise, the customer remains active.

Controls & Validation

The automation does not just produce a heatmap.

The workbook also keeps a customer-level summary and full transaction detail, so I can trace a retention number back to the individual customer and ultimately to the paid invoice behind it.

I also reconcile the totals back to the source data before relying on the analysis. For me, fast code is not useful if I cannot explain where the number came from.

Impact

Instead of rebuilding cohort tables manually each month, the same workflow can be rerun when new data arrives.

More importantly, it changes the analysis from simply asking “How much revenue did we make this month?” to questions such as:

  • Which customer cohorts are retaining better?
  • How quickly does retention fall after acquisition?
  • Is revenue retention stronger or weaker than customer retention?
  • Which products keep customers longer?
  • Where are we seeing expansion or upsell?

The anonymised demo covers roughly three and a half years of monthly data and produces a model where the figures can be traced back to the underlying paid invoices.
