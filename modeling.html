SaaS · ~$6.5M revenue

US B2B Lead-Generation SaaS

The client & their goal

This is a subscription-based lead-generation business providing cold calling and omni-channel outreach through email, LinkedIn and other channels.

The founder runs the business well and wants to keep the option of selling in the future. Our role is to make sure the accounting is clean, but also to understand how the business actually performs and present it in a way that makes sense for this particular model, rather than relying only on standard SaaS metrics.

The challenge

The interesting part is that the business does not behave like a typical SaaS company.

On paper, churn and LTV can look quite poor because many customers leave after around 4 to 5 months. But the service is built to generate campaign results quite quickly, usually within the first few months. If the customer's own campaign does not work, they may stop even when the service itself was delivered as expected.

So the question became whether the standard churn number was really telling us the full story.

What I did

I first built cohort, churn and retention analysis to test the founder's assumption against the actual customer data.

Then I broke the cohort analysis down by product and connected it with product-level margins. This helped us see not only which products generated more revenue, but which ones kept customers longer and produced healthier margins.

For the investor view, I also created an adjusted retention view based on a 3-month threshold, alongside the standard churn analysis. The idea was not to hide churn, but to separate customers who left before the service had enough time to demonstrate its value from customers who left afterwards.

Why it matters

This project reminded me that a metric is only useful when you understand what is behind it.

Whether the founder eventually sells the business or keeps growing it, the starting point is still the same: clean books, consistent definitions and a clear view of how the business really works.

I don't think we should force every company into the same SaaS template. But if we use a different view, the logic needs to be transparent and supported by the data.

E-Commerce · ~A$5.5M revenue

Australian Electric-Heating E-Commerce

The client & their goal

This is a D2C and B2B business importing premium electric radiators and selling through Shopify and a large network of architects across Australia. The owner is targeting an exit by 2027.

The business is highly seasonal. Most of the revenue comes during the Australian winter, while costs continue throughout the year. The owner also follows a profit-first approach, setting aside 4% of monthly revenue as owner profit.

The challenge

This is probably one of the hardest forecasts I work with because several problems happen at the same time.

First, the business earns most of its cash in winter but still needs that cash to survive the quieter summer months.

Second, radiators are bulky and expensive to hold. With around four months of supplier lead time, getting the inventory forecast wrong can become very costly.

And third, low-season cash can become very tight while staffing and other fixed costs do not disappear. The 4% profit allocation makes that even tighter in months where expenses are already higher than revenue.

What I did

My team and I built a detailed budget and cash-flow forecast around the seasonality of the business.

The model was not just there to tell us whether the month was above or below budget. We used it to support actual decisions, such as when the business might need to draw on its line of credit, which supplier payments could potentially be delayed, and how much inventory it could afford to bring in.

We also modelled counter-seasonal products such as ceiling fans to see whether they could reduce the summer revenue gap, while keeping in mind that the owner plans to sell the business and may not have unlimited time to wait for the investment to pay back.

Why it matters

The business is profitable, but profitability alone does not mean the operation underneath is healthy.

What I keep learning from this client is that you need to build the foundation first. Understand the seasonality, inventory, cash cycle and operational constraints before making the bigger decision about growth or exit.

The type of business can change, but for me the FP&A job is still the same: understand how the business really works, then make sure the numbers tell that story clearly.
