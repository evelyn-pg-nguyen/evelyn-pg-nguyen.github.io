Financial Analysis Dashboard

Overview

A personal Power BI project built to explore how sales, forecast, profitability, COGS and discounts can be brought together into one financial performance view.

Why I Built It

This was one of my first Power BI projects. I built it while learning how data visualization could support the kind of budgeting, forecasting and financial analysis work I was becoming more interested in.

The goal was not to make the dashboard as complicated as possible. I wanted to see whether I could take a simple dataset and make the main business questions easy to answer.

Business Questions

The dashboard was designed around a few questions:

  • Are sales on track against forecast?
  • Which products drive the most revenue, and which actually generate the best margin?
  • Where are discounts and COGS putting pressure on profitability?
  • Which months or products need a closer look?

What I Built

I created a two-page Power BI report with an Overview page for high-level performance and a Detail page for deeper product analysis.

The Overview includes KPI cards for gross sales, forecast sales, forecast accuracy, profit margin and discount impact, together with monthly actual vs forecast performance.

The Detail page goes further into product performance, including sales by month, forecast gaps, COGS, discount impact and profitability.

Users can also filter the report by fiscal year, segment and country.

Key Insights

  • Sales finished at $92.31M against a $94.79M forecast, reaching 97.4% of plan. Performance was below forecast in 8 of 12 months, with the largest miss in December.
  • Paseo generated the most sales, but its margin was weaker than several smaller products, showing why revenue alone does not tell the full story.
  • Montana delivered the strongest profit margin, while VTT had the lowest discount impact.
  • The product view makes it easier to separate high-volume products from products that are actually more efficient at converting sales into profit.

What I Learned

This project changed how I think about dashboards. I originally thought a good dashboard needed more visuals and more complexity. Instead, I learned that simple is often better.

A dashboard is useful when someone can look at it, ask “So what?”, and find the answer without needing me to explain every chart.

AI-Built Financial Reporting Dashboard

Overview

This is my first Power BI project built almost entirely with AI.

Using four CSV exports, I worked with Claude to build a five-page financial report covering Revenue, Cost of Sales, Gross Margin, Operating Expenses and Operating Margin. Claude handled the data structure, model, measures, visuals and financial insights. I mainly stepped in to review the logic and give the final design touch.

The goal of this project was not to build the perfect dashboard. I wanted to see what an LLM is already capable of doing inside Power BI today.

Why I Built It

I already knew AI could help with DAX, Power Query and data transformation. What I was not sure about was whether it could go further and actually build the report itself.

So I tested it as part of Anh Leimer's Power BI training.

What surprised me most was how well Claude could understand messy finance data and structure it into something usable. It created the model, relationships and measures, then moved on to building the report pages and explaining what the numbers were saying.

That was much further than I expected.

Business Questions

I wanted the report to answer the type of questions I would normally ask during a monthly FP&A review:

  • What changed this month and why?
  • Which revenue streams are growing or declining?
  • Are changes in Cost of Sales driven by revenue or something unusual?
  • How are Gross Margin and Operating Margin developing?
  • Which accounts, vendors or contractors need attention?
  • Is the current cost base sustainable at the current revenue level?

What I Built

The final report contains five pages:

Revenue
Monthly revenue, 12-month average, revenue by account and contact, and current month versus prior month performance.

Cost of Sales
Monthly Cost of Sales trends, merchant fees and software costs with account-level analysis.

Gross Margin
Gross Profit and Gross Margin trends across the last 12 months.

Operating Expenses
Monthly OPEX with account, contractor and vendor analysis.

Operating Margin
Operating Profit and Operating Margin trends, including monthly profitability and break-even context.

Behind the report is an eight-table data model with around 60 measures.

Claude did most of the heavy lifting here. It was particularly strong at structuring the data and turning the financial logic into a working model.

Design was a different story.

Claude can build a functional dashboard, but I would not say it has much taste yet. I still adjusted the spacing, colours, layout and some visual choices at the end. But considering that most of the report was created from plain-English prompts, I think the starting point was already very good.

Key Insights

The financial insights shown in the dashboard were also generated by Claude from the underlying data.

  • September revenue recovered to $99.8K, up 39.6% from August and above the 12-month average. Private Coaching drove most of the increase and represents a large share of trailing revenue.
  • Cost of Sales increased with the higher revenue, but the main driver was Stripe fees rather than a deterioration in the underlying cost structure. Gross Margin remained strong at 96.2%.
  • Operating Expenses increased only 4.7% to $77.9K, with people-related costs remaining the largest part of the cost base.
  • The combination of stronger revenue and relatively controlled costs moved the business from a $5.8K Operating Loss in August to an $18.1K Operating Profit in September.

What I find interesting is that I did not write these conclusions first and ask AI to display them. Claude worked through the data and produced the analysis itself.

What I Learned

The biggest surprise was not the speed. It was how much of the process AI could already carry.

Claude was very good at structuring the data, creating the financial model and finding patterns in the numbers. It could move from raw exports to a working report and then explain what changed.

Where it was weaker was design judgement. The first version was functional, but not something I would publish without a final human touch.

There were also things I still had to challenge. Some account tables initially showed irrelevant rows, the key messages are currently static rather than dynamically generated, and some data loaded into the model is not yet used in the report.

So I don't see this project as proof that AI can replace a Power BI developer or FP&A analyst.

For me, it shows how much the role is changing.

AI can already do a large part of the building. What still matters is knowing what question to ask, whether the logic makes sense, whether the insight is useful and whether the final report is something another person can actually understand.

This was my first AI-built Power BI project, and more than anything, I wanted it to show what is already possible with LLMs today.
